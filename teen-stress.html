Stress is normal. Too much of it, for too long, is not.

A deadline that makes your heart race can actually help you perform. The same feeling, every day, for months, is a different story.

Teen Stress Social Media

Stress is your body's normal response to a demanding situation. A good dose of it before a game, a presentation, or a test can actually motivate you and sharpen your focus. The problem is not stress itself. It's stress that never lets up, or that becomes bigger than what you can handle, that can start to wear on your wellbeing.

Common sources of teen stress

Everyone's mix is different, but these come up again and again.

Schoolwork and exams

Deadlines and test pressure, especially when they stack up.

Grades

Worrying about results, or about living up to a number.

Parents' expectations

Wanting to make the people you love proud, sometimes at a cost.

Friendships

Conflict, distance, or just not knowing where you stand.

Relationships

Dating, breakups, and the emotions that come with both.

Social media

More on this just below, because it's complicated.

Peer pressure

Feeling like you have to act, look, or choose a certain way.

Bullying

In person or online, being targeted takes a real toll.

Social comparison

Measuring your life against everyone else's highlight reel.

Pressure about the future

Big decisions about courses, careers, or what comes after high school.

Changes during adolescence

Your body, your friendships, and your identity are all shifting at once.

School pressure in Canada

In 2002, 21% of Grade 10 girls in Canada (and 16% of boys) reported feeling a lot of pressure from schoolwork[source]. That's the most recent figure we could fully verify for this exact question. More recent Canadian survey data exists, but we could not confirm an exact, up to date percentage for schoolwork pressure, so we are not going to guess at a number. What is clear across the research is that school pressure has consistently been one of the most commonly reported stressors for Canadian teens, in 2002 and still today.

Teens who already have worse mental health might also turn to media more, as a way to cope or avoid other things. The link goes both directions, and the Canadian data can't separate which comes first.

The highlight reel problem

We often compare our real life to other people's highlight reels. A feed is a curated best-of, not a documentary. That comparison, done over and over, can quietly chip away at how you feel about your own ordinary, unfiltered day.

What gets posted: the best five minutes of the week.

What doesn't get posted: basically everything else.

Benefits and risks, honestly

Benefits

  • Staying in touch with friends, even far away
  • Finding communities around things you care about
  • A space to express yourself and be creative

Risks

  • Constant social comparison
  • Cyberbullying
  • Lost sleep from late-night scrolling
  • Near-constant use crowding out other things
What the research actually supports Research has found associations between certain patterns of social media use and poorer mental health outcomes, but the relationship is complex. It does not show that social media causes depression.

Stress check-in

Six quick questions. Answer honestly, there's no wrong answer, and nothing you type is saved or sent anywhere.

0 of 6 answered

1. How has your sleep been this week?

2. How are things with schoolwork or deadlines right now?

3. How connected do you feel to the people around you?

4. How often do you feel tense or on edge?

5. How is your energy for things you usually enjoy?

6. All together, how much feels like it's going on in your life right now?

This is not a medical test. It's just a tool to help you reflect. Nothing you answer here is stored or sent anywhere, it disappears the moment you leave the page.
