What is mental health, really?

Not a label, and not a mood. It is one part of your overall health, the same way your body is.

Mental health covers your emotional, psychological, and social well-being. It shapes how you think and feel, how you handle stress, how you relate to other people, and how you deal with the everyday challenges that come your way.

It changes over your lifetime, and it changes week to week too. Feeling stressed, sad, or anxious sometimes does not automatically mean you have a mental health disorder. Those feelings are a normal part of being human. What matters more is how long they last, and how much they get in the way of your life.

Why adolescence matters

The teen years are a major period of physical, emotional, and social development. This is when you are building the skills you will use for the rest of your life: managing emotions, solving problems, building relationships, and coping with stress. That makes adolescence an especially important window for mental health, for better and for worse.

What can influence mental health?

There is no single cause. It is usually a mix of factors, and having more of these at once can increase the effect[source]. Tap any factor to read more.

How stable, supportive, or tense things are at home shapes a lot of how safe and steady you feel day to day.
Close, trusted friendships are one of the strongest protective factors there is. Shaky or missing friendships can do the opposite.
Whether school feels safe and fair, or stressful and unpredictable, adds up over a full school year.
Grades, exams, and deadlines are a normal source of pressure, but when they never let up, they can wear a person down.
Pressure to conform with peers, to act a certain way or fit in, is named by the WHO as a real factor in teen mental health[source].
Figuring out who you are, including things like gender, culture, and values, is a core part of adolescence and can be its own kind of pressure.
Being targeted, whether in person or online, is strongly linked to anxiety, low mood, and feeling unsafe at school.
Its effects are complex and depend a lot on how it is used. There is a whole section on this on the Teen Stress & Social Media page.
Money stress, housing instability, or community conditions at home can add a steady background level of pressure.
Exposure to difficult or traumatic experiences is one of the clearest risk factors the WHO points to[source].
